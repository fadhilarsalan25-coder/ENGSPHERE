What EngSphere is

A self-paced English tutor that fits in one browser tab. It takes the structure of a proper language course — a syllabus, graded practice, feedback on every answer — and strips out the scheduling, the fees, and the waiting for a teacher to mark your work.

Built for the learner who's stuck in the middle

Most people learning English aren't complete beginners and aren't fluent either. They know some words, half-remember the tenses, and freeze when it's time to actually produce a sentence. Textbooks explain too much at once; apps drill vocabulary but never explain why a sentence is wrong.

EngSphere sits between the two. Read a short rule, see it used in a real sentence, then answer questions about it — and get told exactly why your answer was right or wrong before moving on.

The three-step loop

  1. Read a rule written in plain language, with its formula and examples.
  2. Practise it in a quiz set to your level.
  3. Review the explanation for every answer, then earn XP and keep your streak.

Repeat on a topic until it stops feeling like guessing. That's the whole method.

English tenses are organised by time (present, past, future) and aspect (simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous). Tap a card to see its formula, usage and examples.

The verb to be (am / is / are / was / were) is the most common verb in English. It shows identity, description and location, and it also builds continuous and passive sentences.

Present: am / is / are

SubjectFormExample
IamI am a student.
You / We / TheyareThey are ready.
He / She / ItisShe is at work.

Past: was / were

SubjectFormExample
I / He / She / ItwasHe was tired.
You / We / TheywereWe were at home.

Negative forms

Subject + be + not

Contract naturally in speech: isn't, aren't, wasn't, weren't. "It is not far" → "It isn't far."

Question forms

Be + subject + …?

Move the be-verb before the subject: "You are ready." → "Are you ready?" · "She was late." → "Was she late?"

Four everyday jobs of "to be"

  • Identity — "I am Maria."
  • Description — "The soup is hot."
  • Location — "They are in Jakarta."
  • Existence — "There is a problem." / "There are two options."

Building block for other grammar

Continuous tenses: be + verb-ing → "I am learning."  ·  Passive voice: be + past participle → "The letter was sent."

Grammar is just the set of patterns that hold a sentence together. Start with the building blocks (parts of speech), then the order they go in.

Parts of speech

Noun

A person, place, thing or idea.

teacher, Jakarta, freedom

Verb

An action or state.

run, is, believe

Adjective

Describes a noun.

a quiet street

Adverb

Describes a verb, adjective, or another adverb.

she runs quickly

Pronoun

Replaces a noun.

he, they, it, this

Preposition

Shows relation (place, time, direction).

on, at, since, between

Conjunction

Joins words or clauses.

and, but, because

Article

Marks a noun as specific or general.

a, an, the

Basic sentence structure — SVO

Subject + Verb + Object

English word order is fixed: "She (S) reads (V) books (O)." Adjectives usually sit before the noun; adverbs of time/place usually go at the end.

Subject–verb agreement

A singular subject takes a singular verb, and a plural subject takes a plural verb: "He runs" vs. "They run."

A, an, or the?

a / an introduce something not yet specific ("a dog", "an apple" — use an before a vowel sound). the points to something already known or unique ("the sun", "the dog we saw").

Making a sentence negative or a question

Most verbs need a helper: do / does / did. "I like tea" → "I don't like tea." → "Do you like tea?"
